History

Te Puia in Rotorua, New Zealand, sits within the Whakarewarewa Valley, which was first occupied around 1325 as a strongly fortified Māori pā. Built beneath the cliffs of Pohaturoa Mountain and protected by a natural moat of hot pools, the valley served as an impenetrable stronghold that never fell in battle, while local iwi learned to utilize the rich geothermal energy for cooking, healing, and warmth.

In the 1920s, leader Sir Āpirana Ngata helped establish early efforts to protect traditional carving practices, eventually leading to the passing of the New Zealand Māori Arts and Crafts Institute Act in 1963. This institute was founded on the current site to foster and preserve ancestral Māori arts like weaving and woodcarving, merging indigenous cultural education with the region's long-standing tourism heritage.

In recent history, the mandate to protect these traditions evolved when Parliament enacted the New Zealand Māori Arts and Crafts Institute Vesting Act in 2020, returning ownership of Te Puia directly to local mana whenua iwi partners. Today, it remains a world-famous cultural and geothermal center centered around the massive Pōhutu Geyser.
